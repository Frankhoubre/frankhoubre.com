**The New York Times** is asking its freelance photographers not to use the latest version of **Photoshop**. The reason: some features in the 2027 release use AI "in ways that may not be apparent to users," and can leave **metadata** saying the photo is AI-generated.

It's the first time I've seen a major newsroom refuse an update over this. And the problem isn't what photographers do with AI. It's what the software writes into the file without them noticing.

This piece is based on [Emma Roth's article for The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1041227/the-new-york-times-warns-photographers-not-to-update-photoshop-over-ai-features), published on November 24 and read on November 25, 2026. I haven't seen the full email, and Adobe's release notes page wouldn't open from my reading tool.

## Where the problem comes from

Since August, Adobe has been adding **C2PA** metadata across its apps. It records whether an image was generated or edited with AI features. In principle, that's a good thing: I wrote about it when Adobe suggested [labeling the original rather than the AI](/en/blog/c2pa-etiqueter-originalite-pas-ia-adobe).

The catch is that it's no longer clear which tool counts as "AI." The Verge notes that even older options, like automated selection, may now be considered part of the AI suite. The November update also adds an **AI Assisted Editor** view that gathers every AI feature in one toolbar, a step-by-step editing assistant, prompt-based editing, new AI upscaling options and an AI tool that recomposes images for other canvas sizes.

So a photographer adjusting a selection can end up with a photo tagged as AI-edited. For the Times, which says it does not "retouch or digitally manipulate" photos beyond minimal color adjustments, that's a direct risk to reader trust.

The Verge says it's still unclear which specific features the Times means, and Adobe hadn't responded when the article was published.

## What I make of it

The decision makes sense to me, and it should worry Adobe a little. It makes sense because an AI tag on a news photo, even a mistaken one, costs more than any time saved. It's worrying because labeling was meant to build trust, and now it's pushing away professionals who don't generate anything.

The real issue is **clarity**. A tool that marks your files should tell you plainly when and why it does it. Until it does, demanding clients will prefer to stay on an older version.

## What it means for you

- **Know what your tools write into your files.** Check an export's metadata (with a C2PA verifier or an EXIF reader) before delivering to a sensitive client.
- **Read your clients' rules.** Newsrooms, institutions and photo contests will increasingly set rules on allowed versions and tools.
- **Don't update mid-job.** Keep a stable version for the length of a project and test the new one on the side.
- **Separate your uses.** For reportage, minimal editing and no generative features. For creative work, use AI openly and say so. I've laid out the line between [retouching, inpainting and generation in photography](/en/blog/ia-photographie-retouche-inpainting-generation).

## FAQ

### Why is The New York Times advising against Photoshop 2027?

Because some features use AI in ways that may not be apparent and can produce metadata identifying photos as AI-generated, according to the email sent to its freelance photographers.

### Which Photoshop versions are affected?

Photoshop 2027, release 28.0.0, and all later updates, according to the email quoted by The Verge.

### What is C2PA metadata?

Information embedded in a file that describes its origin and edits, including the use of AI. Adobe began rolling it out across its apps in August 2026.

### What AI features does Photoshop 2027 add?

An AI Assisted Editor view that gathers the AI features, a step-by-step editing assistant, prompt-based editing, new AI upscaling options and an AI recompose tool for other canvas sizes, according to The Verge.

### Did the Times confirm the rule?

Yes. Spokesperson Graham James confirmed it to The Verge.

### How can I tell if my photos carry an AI label?

Inspect your exports' metadata with a C2PA verifier or an EXIF reader before delivering them, especially to a news client.
